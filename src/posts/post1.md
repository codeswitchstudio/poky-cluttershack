My favourite bookshop, Libreria, held a literary fest called Typographia: the Future of Print. It was a sunny end-of-summer Saturday, and the Second Home rooftop was already packed, with a long line of people still waiting for the lift to catch Geoff Dyer.

The event was billed as “Beyond Genre: a conversation about the freedom of not knowing what kind of book you’re writing, and what becomes possible when fiction, memoir, criticism, travel writing and essay start to blur.”

I was curious. I had read Jeff in Venice, Death in Varanasi, some of his essays in Reading the Room, and more on his travels in Yoga for People Who Can’t Be Bothered to Do It. Products of their time I associate with an aspiration I think more of us uppity-middle-class young artsy media types in late-90/00s Metro Manila would have cared to admit: that we wanted to be privileged bohemian burgis first-world hedonist-philosophers in the same third world playground that backpackers and trustafarians saw, and othered us with. Later, some of us joined the diaspora, searching for if not reinventing ourselves to thrive in someone else’s greener turf.

“Remember to write the book only you can write!” says a note-to-self post-it on the wall of the room where Mr Dyer (now 68, silver-haired and still standing tall) writes. This is how he described it to us in the audience.

Memory, obviously, is not always the same for everyone. The colour of a Rolls-Royce in one of his books was not actually yellow, according to a dispute with a relative. The conversation went on to how he made a book about writing, tried to write about D.H. Lawrence, and then ended up writing about failing to write about D.H. Lawrence. He mentioned the narrative prose writer Annie Dillard, who had just passed away a few days before. He also talked about class in his memoir Homework: how grammar school led him to the very different world of Oxford, which led to his success being celebrated at this book fair.

He acknowledged his white-male privilege, and how that may not allow him to write about the same things today as he did back then. It was so much easier at the time to write about the history of jazz in But Beautiful without being questioned for not being a Black person in the scene, or ever having been to America.

(This makes me think of water-cooler moment in a London office some years back, with two guys and a girl from California. Someone mentioned fish sauce, and the Northern guy turned to the Californian and asked what she’d recommend from Southeast Asia, since she had backpacked there and so of course she would know. I was standing right there, a Filipino, holding a tea biscuit, while she went on about full moon parties, Sagada, Palawan and tribal tattoos. Oohs, aah, and before I could say anything, everyone had to get back to work. It was not a great feeling, not being asked.)

At the end of it all was Q&A time. “What works?” seemed to be the common question from the young people in the audience with an appetite for bestseller success. I forget what he had said by then. “Remember to write the book only you can write!” Mr Dyer had already said earlier, and that is the thing I took away from this talk.
